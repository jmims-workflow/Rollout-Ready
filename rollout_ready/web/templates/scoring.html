How it works

A 2 minute read on how Rollout Ready decides whether a prompt change is ready for your team.

What the app does

A prompt change can look fine when you try it once. Rollout Ready runs the version you're trying on a small set of saved examples, scores every output, and compares it with the version your team uses now. You get one answer, and a list of the examples that got worse.

An example is only an input: what someone on your team would paste in. Nobody writes the ideal answer.

How the AI reviewer scores

The AI reviewer is Claude. It reads one output at a time and scores it from 1 to 5 on each quality in what good looks like, with a one-sentence reason for every score. An example's score is the average of those.

It scores blind: it is never told which version wrote the output, and it never sees the other version's output, so it can't take sides. Quick checks run too: plain rules like a word limit, which are free and always give the same answer.

Why one broken example matters more than the average

A change can lift the average while quietly breaking the examples you care about. In the demo, version 3 of the follow-up email raised the average from 3.6 to 3.9, but two emails stopped answering the customer's questions. So the answer is "Not ready to roll out."

Each example is marked:

  • Got worse: its score dropped by half a point or more, it fails a quick check it used to pass, or it newly misses a deal-breaker.
  • Got better: its score rose by half a point or more, or it passes a quick check it used to fail.
  • About the same: anything smaller. Every run varies a little, so small moves don't count.

Then the answer is:

  • Nothing got worse and at least one got better: Ready to roll out, unless a deal-breaker is missed.
  • Nothing got worse or better: No clear difference.
  • Some got worse, but more got better: Not ready to roll out. Fix the ones that got worse first.
  • Some got worse, and no more got better: Keep the current version.

Deal-breakers

Some qualities matter more than the rest: getting the facts right, or answering every question. Mark those as deal-breakers. If the AI reviewer scores a deal-breaker {{ cutoff }} or lower on any example, the version you're trying can't be "Ready to roll out", whatever the average says. If it newly misses one, that example counts as Got worse.

Most prompts have one or two deal-breakers. If everything is a deal-breaker, nothing is.

Why you can disagree with the AI reviewer

The AI reviewer is a model, and it can be wrong. You know your customers and your team. On any example, choose "I disagree with the AI reviewer", say what your call is, and give a one-line reason.

Your call changes how that example counts for those two versions only, and the answer is worked out again. The AI reviewer's scores are never changed, and the result says which examples you overrode and why, so anyone you share it with can see.

What a good example mix looks like

Most prompts work on everyday inputs. They break on the hard ones. Six to eight examples is plenty if they include:

  • Everyday: a typical input, the kind the team pastes in most days.
  • Messy or incomplete: rough notes, missing details, or things out of order.
  • Complicated: several people, several asks, or details that are easy to mix up.
  • Broke before: an input where a real output went wrong. When that happens, add it, with names and details changed.

How much a result proves

Every result says how many examples it is based on, and reminds you: even when a version is ready, spot-check real outputs in its first week.

Six to eight good examples catch most breakages. They can't prove a prompt will never break, because your team will paste in things nobody thought to test. Rollout Ready makes a change much safer, not risk-free.
